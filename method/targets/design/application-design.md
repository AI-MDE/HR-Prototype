## Purpose

What a good design looks like, and how to check it. Design turns the requirements into the realization the team builds from, within the selected architecture and stack, without being source code. Its shape is defined by the [Design](../../metamodel/design/README.md) concepts; the facets have their own Targets: [UI design](ui-design.md), [API design](api-design.md), [persistence design](persistence-design.md).

It governs: [Design](../../metamodel/design/README.md) (design semantics on the owning concepts), the capability design index, use-case realization, Entity Designs, and sample data.

## Composed behavior

### Optional Feature realization  `[feature: application-design-aspect-realization]`

The requirements may apply an optional Feature, with its options; the design says how the chosen runtime or stack realizes it. The Method's [catalog](../../optional-features/index.md) defines each Feature's meaning, parameters, known realizations and promises; it does not promise that every Feature has an implementation. For each optional Feature in scope, record in the application's [catalog](../../metamodel/design/design-catalog.md) the realization chosen and the one component that implements it, and identify:

- the requirement and the concepts or operations it applies to;
- the implementation mechanism and its owning component, including how the application reaches it;
- configuration and defaults, required model or storage contributions (`adds`), and where it is invoked in the operations;
- API and UI behavior, authorization, failure handling, and its stated constraints with the operation and with other Features (*Before*, *After*, *Atomic*, *After commit*);
- the scenarios that prove each of its promises, on each object that applies it.

A design may apply an optional Feature the requirements did not ask for, as a technical choice, when its `appliedIn` includes `design`; that is recorded in the capability design, not in the requirements. Reference an existing shared implementation rather than duplicating it per entity. Check actual support in the selected runtime or stack: a catalog entry or a checkbox is not support. Where support is missing, specify the reusable capability or application implementation needed. Known gaps and evidence of support are tracked in Work. A design for future implementation is not evidence that the Feature works.

When the stack's Architecture declares an invocation convention (where an entity's implementation lives, and how a Feature call is marked), follow it; `mde verify` checks it ([Architecture](../../metamodel/architecture/README.md)).

### Capability design index  `[feature: application-design-capability-design-index]`

It records the capability's primary entity, its module boundary (what it owns and what it consumes from other capabilities), and references to its entities, use cases, and pages. It is an index, not a re-narration: operations live on entities, behavior in use cases, contracts in API Contracts, pages in their own specs. Restating them creates two copies that drift apart.

### Policy stays in Requirements  `[feature: application-design-policy-stays-in-requirements]`

Business policy is requirements: the allocation limit and the meaning of overlapping dates are requirements; the calculation algorithm, the atomic validate-and-write mechanism, and how a conflict is presented are design. A missing permission, deletion policy, or business exception is a requirements gap, never a technical choice for design to invent. Selected realizations go in Specs; alternatives still under consideration, open questions, evidence status, and decision history go in Work, linked to the Specs, and concepts never refer back to that tracking.

### Use-case realization  `[feature: application-design-use-case-realization]`

Capture how a use case is realized in its own design semantics or in the Pages, API Contracts, Data Model, and Integrations it references. Resolve operations, inputs and context, transaction and concurrency boundaries, authorization enforcement, error outcomes, interaction bindings, and asynchronous or waiting behavior where relevant.

### Verifying requirements and design  `[feature: application-design-verifying-requirements-and-design]`

Verification checks that requirements and design are complete, consistent, and executable enough to realize the business behavior. It never adds design semantics just to make generation convenient: when it finds missing information, update the requirement or design that owns it.

- **A business rule** is verified against representative data, preferably the sample data: start from business objects and a proposed change or operation, navigate declared relationships to the related records, filter, aggregate, or derive the values the rule needs, evaluate it, and return an observable business result. Never verify a rule by passing precomputed totals or hand-picked related records the model should derive. Cover normal, rejection, boundary, historical or inactive, and exception cases. If the model cannot supply what the rule needs, that is a model or design gap.
- **A use case** is complete when it can be performed end to end from the specifications: the authorized actor can start it; its business objects can be identified or selected; required attributes can be supplied or derived; relationships can be traversed; the entity operations exist; its rules can execute against the model; the state change or outcome can be represented; the designed page, action, and navigation carry it out when there is a UI; and success and expected failures are observable.
- **A gap** is diagnostic: identify the owning concept, refine its requirement or design, and verify again. Never solve a gap by duplicating semantics into another artifact.

### What design does  `[feature: application-design-what-design-does]`

- Determine how the requested requirements are realized within the selected architecture and stack: reason about alternatives, choose a realization, and check that it preserves business meaning. Design is more than filling outlines or describing code after it is written.
- Design only what the requested scope needs. An operation can use existing runtime capabilities and existing design decisions without a new service, endpoint, or document. Prefer declarative realization where the runtime can interpret the knowledge; ordinary application code is the escape hatch.
- A useful design identifies the requirement or operation it realizes, the chosen realization and why, the affected contracts or components, and the failure and boundary behavior needed to verify it. Reuse or update existing decisions rather than copying them into competing definitions.

### Design in Features  `[feature: design-in-catalog-terms]`

- Write the catalog as you design: start it with the first design work, add a Feature the first time a design applies it, and reuse it from then on. Use the Method's [optional Features](../../optional-features/index.md) by id, with this application's realization, rather than inventing a synonym; define an application Feature only when none fits.
- Apply a Feature for a reusable behavior with promises worth proving on each object that applies it; the design vocabulary (`sets`, `delegates`, `locks`… for operations; `unique`, `index`, `check`, `label` for entities) for how an operation is composed. Never invent a Feature to hold one operation's own design.
- An operation's own design (its inputs and outputs, what it records, the operations it calls, its transaction and failure behavior) is a short entry in its entity's [Entity Design](../../metamodel/design/entity-design.md). A choice that needs a reason gets one line there; a choice across the application is an architecture decision.
- What every operation does the same way (one transaction, inputs checked against the model, the standard errors) is the architecture's, stated once: neither a Feature applied to each operation nor a default.
- Write nothing that is absent: no Feature means the default, or none. A default (for the application or every entity) is in the catalog's `defaults`, never repeated in a design.
- State a fact once, at its source: a cascade in the entity's Relationships, a business rule in the requirements, who may do what in the [Authorization](../../metamodel/design/authorization.md). The design only realizes them.
- One Feature per meaning: two ids for one meaning, or one id for two, is a defect.
- Realize every optional Feature the requirements apply to an entity, by the capability design or a default; never apply one whose `appliedIn` is `[requirements]` that the requirements did not ask for.

### Engineering for reuse  `[feature: engineering-for-reuse]`

- An application Feature (one the Method does not have) is described for people: its `description` says, in plain words, what it gives, so a designer or reviewer understands a design without reading code. A Method Feature's description is the Method's.
- Prove each applied Feature's promises on each object that applies it: a scenario tagged `@feature:<id>/<promise>`, with the operation it performs, exercises what the promise says, through the API or, for a structural Feature, against the migrated test database, and passes. A Feature with a promise unproven on an object that applies it is not done there, whatever the code says.

### Meaningful sample and seed data  `[feature: meaningful-seed-data]`

Sample data is a concrete design output, not a plan: a small, coherent set of fictional records that makes the application understandable and usable in a preview or prototype. It is instance data, not new entity definitions or business policy.

- Meaningful business labels and realistic values, not placeholder ids as display names.
- Valid relationships and enough connected records to demonstrate the scoped entities, related collections, and use cases.
- Representative lifecycle states and normal and boundary cases the model supports, without inventing states or permissions to fill a screen.
- Inputs for rejection and error scenarios kept apart, so the normal dataset stays consistent with the model.
- A documented way to view it and, with a prototype, to load or reset it repeatably in the development environment, without replacing unrelated records.

The sample data lives in one file, `app/data/seed.json`: an object keyed by entity name exactly as its spec titles it (`Professional`, `ClientContact`, never `professionals`), each a list of records whose fields are the entity's attribute names, references by the related record's `id`. The MDE runtime and the Workbench's live data read this file, so the data can be browsed as soon as it is written; the application's own seed loads the same records (its loader reads this file, or is derived from it). A database or UI is not a prerequisite. Never say the data can be browsed until it is in this file, in this shape. Identify fictional data as such and keep it apart from operational data. Keep the samples in step with entity, relationship, rule, and design changes, and reuse them in prototypes and verification rather than keeping divergent copies. Assumptions the examples expose stay in Work; the examples do not settle them.

### Traceability to business specs  `[feature: traceability-to-business-specs]`

Design is the bridge from requirements to code, not a new source of truth.

- Every design artifact traces to the requirement it realizes: a capability, entity, use case, or business rule. Design entities match the business entities they implement; business logic stays in the domain, not in the UI or persistence.
- A use case's trace runs out of its `usecase` block: attach to a step by its step id, never its sequence number, and resolve each step's `operation.target` to a declared entity operation or page. A step never references a business rule; rules govern the operation it invokes, and rule coverage comes from execution evidence.
- Design may resolve references and add each condition's test layers, but never silently changes a use case's actor, intent, steps, or a condition's situation or expected result. If behavior must change, change the use case first.
- A "traces to" line is not traceability: the artifact must actually mirror what it cites (names, fields, operations, behavior).
